Keyboard & mouse diagnostics guide

A browser tester can confirm that the operating system is delivering your key presses and mouse clicks. It cannot tell you whether a switch is worn out or a cable is intermittent. This guide explains how to use the tester as the first step of a real diagnosis, and what to do when it points at a problem.

How to check whether a single key responds

Start with the most specific test instead of typing a long text. Open the Keyboard Test tab, click once on the page so it has focus, and then press only the key you suspect. A working key lights up green immediately and adds one to both the total presses and the unique keys counters.

If the key lights up but nothing happens in your application, the fault is almost certainly not hardware: it is an application-level remapping, a language layout that moves the character to another key, or a stuck modifier key. If the key never lights up, keep reading below.

Why a key is not detected in the browser

When a key stays dark, the event never reached the page. These are the usual reasons, in the order they are worth checking:

  • The operating system reserves the key. System shortcuts (Windows key, Ctrl+Alt+Delete, some Fn combinations) are handled before any application sees them. No website can detect them, and that is by design.
  • Another program grabs it. Hotkey tools, macro utilities, clipboard managers and RGB drivers install global hooks. While such a tool owns the key, the browser only sees the remaining ones. Close those utilities and test again.
  • A modifier is logically stuck. A held Ctrl, Alt or Shift changes what the system delivers, and some combinations are swallowed. KEYCHECK shows the modifiers it currently believes are held, right under the keyboard, so you can check whether a stuck modifier is the cause.
  • The keyboard is partially disconnected. Wireless keyboards drop out of range and some USB hubs power down individual ports. A key that works when you wiggle the cable or move the receiver closer points to the connection rather than the key.
  • The layout is not what you think. KEYCHECK identifies keys by physical position (KeyboardEvent.code), so it always highlights the same physical key. If the character printed on the key differs from the character you get, you are on a different keyboard layout — change the layout in the operating system, not in the tester.

Dealing with several dead keys

One dead key is usually a mapping or a shortcut. A cluster of dead keys — a whole row, a whole column, or everything on the left half of the keyboard — points to something physical: a membrane contact that has lost contact, a spilled drink, or a keyboard that is partially detached from its case.

Open the tester and press the keys in a grid pattern, skipping the suspect area. If the pattern of unresponsive keys is geometric, the problem is in the matrix and no software will fix it. Clean the keyboard, reseat the ribbon cable, and test on another USB port before considering a replacement.

How to test the mouse buttons

Switch to the Mouse Test tab and move the pointer into the test area. Each button lights up as you press it, and the counters on the right of the box tell you how many times each button was used.

  • Left button — the primary click. If it does not register, first rule out the application: many programs bind their own shortcuts.
  • Middle button — the wheel click. It opens links in a new tab, pans a canvas, or starts autoscroll depending on the software.
  • Right button — the context menu. Inside the test area the context menu is suppressed so the click can be counted; everywhere else on the page it behaves normally.
  • Side buttons (4 and 5) — available on many gaming and productivity mice. They only light up if your mouse actually has them and the operating system forwards them.

A mouse that works on one USB port but not another is a driver or port problem, not a switch problem. A mouse that only loses the side buttons after a sleep/wake cycle is a known behaviour of some wireless receivers and is fixed by re-pairing the device.

How to detect a double-click problem

A worn mouse switch often produces two separate single clicks instead of one double-click. The symptom is classic: you double-click a link and it opens one tab, or the file does not open at all.

KEYCHECK measures the time between two consecutive clicks on the same button and displays it in milliseconds:

  • Two clicks well under 500 ms are reported as a double-click — your hardware and the system agree.
  • Two clicks near 500 ms are reported as a slow double-click. The click may be rejected by applications that use a stricter threshold.
  • Two clicks that are never grouped mean the system does not receive a second click at all — a switch, connection or driver problem.

The double-click threshold itself is configurable in the operating system (Windows: Settings, Bluetooth & devices, Mouse, Additional mouse settings, Double-click speed; macOS: System Settings, Mouse, Click speed). If a slow double-click works when you lower the threshold, your mouse is fine and the setting was simply too strict for your hand speed.

When the scroll wheel behaves strangely

Modern optical scroll wheels are high-resolution: they report a stream of small wheel events instead of one event per notch. The scroll distance is therefore controlled by the operating system and the application, not by the mouse. A wheel that scrolls too fast usually needs the "lines per notch" setting adjusted, while a wheel that skips in both directions points to dust in the encoder or a failing optical sensor — clean it with compressed air and avoid blowing dust deeper into the mechanism.

Pointer that drifts, jumps or moves on its own

Drift is a sensor problem: a dirty or scratched surface, a low battery, or a mouse that is not supported by the current driver. Movement that happens without any physical input is usually caused by an unstable surface, a vibrating desk, or interference with another wireless device on the same frequency band.

The pointer travel counter in the mouse test is useful here: move the mouse a known distance and check whether the pixels reported by the system match what you expect. A large mismatch suggests that the sensor resolution has been changed in the driver settings.

Wireless keyboards and mice that drop out

  1. Replace or recharge the battery. Low battery is the most common cause of erratic behaviour.
  2. Move the receiver closer and away from USB hubs, which can starve devices of power.
  3. Re-pair the device instead of only re-plugging the receiver — the pairing record is what breaks.
  4. Check for interference: 2.4 GHz receivers sit close to Wi-Fi antennas and USB 3 ports.
  5. Update or reinstall the manufacturer driver; many odd behaviours are driver bugs.

What a browser tester can never tell you

  • Whether a key feels physically the same as it used to — the switch could be on its last legs while the event still arrives.
  • Whether the mouse sensor is accurate at high movement speeds, which matters for games but not for the event stream.
  • Whether the keyboard firmware has a broken matrix, when the operating system still translates the key into something else.
  • Whether a USB port or a cable is about to fail. The browser only sees the final event.
Rule of thumb: if the browser tester shows the key or the button working, the path between the switch and the operating system is fine. The remaining suspects are the application, the driver, the operating system configuration and the physical hardware itself.
